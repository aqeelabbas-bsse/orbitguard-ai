Maneuver Decision Criteria

Deciding whether to perform an avoidance maneuver is a tradeoff, not a purely
automatic reaction to any flagged conjunction. Operators generally weigh:

1. Collision risk level — driven by miss distance, relative velocity, and
   the sizes of both objects, since tracking uncertainty means a "predicted"
   miss distance carries a margin of error that shrinks as the event gets
   closer in time and tracking data updates.
2. Consequence if wrong — a collision with a large object is far more
   costly than one with a small fragment, both in immediate mission impact
   and in long-term debris generation.
3. Cost of the maneuver itself — every maneuver spends fuel (delta-v),
   which is a finite, non-renewable resource for most missions, and may
   also disrupt planned mission activities or scheduling.
4. Time to closest approach — more lead time allows for cheaper, smaller
   corrective maneuvers and more opportunity to re-assess as tracking
   improves; last-minute decisions typically require larger, costlier burns.

A reasonable general decision pattern: low-severity events are monitored and
re-assessed as new tracking data arrives rather than acted on immediately;
medium-severity events warrant a maneuver review, weighing the fuel cost
against the risk reduction achieved; high-severity events (very close
approach, high relative velocity, or a large object involved) generally
warrant proceeding with a maneuver unless the fuel cost is prohibitively high
relative to remaining mission life.
