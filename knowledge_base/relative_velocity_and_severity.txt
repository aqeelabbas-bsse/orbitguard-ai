Relative Velocity and Collision Severity

Relative velocity between two objects at closest approach affects risk in two
separate ways, and it is worth treating them separately when assessing an
event.

First, it affects how much tracking uncertainty matters. Objects are tracked
with some position uncertainty that grows the further out the prediction is
made. At high relative velocity, a small timing error translates into a
larger positional shift at the predicted closest-approach point, which
widens the effective uncertainty around the predicted miss distance itself.

Second, it affects consequence. Impact energy in a collision scales with the
square of relative velocity, so a collision at high relative velocity is
dramatically more destructive, and more likely to generate a large debris
field, than one at low relative velocity — even at the same combined mass.

In low Earth orbit, relative velocities between objects in similar orbital
planes are often modest, but objects in different orbital planes or opposing
directions (for example, an object in a near-polar orbit crossing a
near-equatorial one) can have very high relative velocities even though both
objects individually move at typical orbital speeds. This is why relative
velocity should always be assessed alongside miss distance, not as an
afterthought — a moderate miss distance at very high relative velocity can
warrant more urgency than a smaller miss distance at low relative velocity.
