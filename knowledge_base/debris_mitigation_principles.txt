Debris Mitigation Principles

International space debris mitigation practice, broadly reflected in
guidelines from bodies such as the UN Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer
Space and major space agencies, centers on a few core ideas: minimize the
creation of new debris during normal operations, avoid intentional
break-ups, limit the probability of accidental collisions during a mission's
operational life, and where possible plan for controlled deorbit or disposal
at end of life so objects do not linger as long-term collision risks.

For an operator monitoring a fleet, the practical implication is that
avoiding a collision is not just about protecting one's own spacecraft — a
collision generates a debris field that raises risk for every other object
sharing that orbital region for years afterward. This is why even a
moderate-probability event involving a large object (an active satellite or
a substantial debris fragment) is often treated more seriously than a
higher-probability event involving a very small fragment, since the
consequence of a large-object collision is disproportionately worse for the
broader orbital environment.

This context matters for maneuver decisions: a small avoidance burn that
costs some fuel is almost always preferable to accepting elevated collision
risk, given how costly debris-generating collisions are to the entire
orbital environment, not just the two objects involved.
