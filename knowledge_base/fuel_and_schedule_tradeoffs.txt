Fuel and Schedule Tradeoffs in Maneuver Planning

Every avoidance maneuver spends propellant, and for most spacecraft that
propellant budget is fixed for the life of the mission — there is no
refueling once it runs low. This makes fuel a genuine scarce resource that
has to be weighed against collision risk on every decision, not treated as
free insurance.

A useful way to frame the tradeoff: the "cost" of a maneuver is not just the
immediate delta-v spent, it is that delta-v's share of the total remaining
budget, since spending fuel now on a marginal-risk event reduces the
spacecraft's ability to respond to a more severe event later in its mission
life or to perform planned end-of-life disposal maneuvers.

Schedule impact matters too. A maneuver can shift a spacecraft's ground
track, delay planned observations or communications windows, or require
follow-up trim maneuvers to return to the nominal mission orbit — all of
which have operational cost beyond the fuel itself.

In general, the smallest maneuver that achieves an adequate risk reduction is
preferable to a larger one, both because it conserves fuel and because
smaller burns are typically easier to plan accurately with less schedule
disruption. Choosing between maneuver options should weigh risk reduction
achieved per unit of fuel spent, not simply which option produces the
largest final miss distance.
