Background: Cosmos 1408 Debris Field

In November 2021, a direct-ascent anti-satellite missile test destroyed the
defunct Soviet-era satellite Cosmos 1408, which had been in low Earth orbit
since the 1980s. The test created a large field of trackable debris
fragments spread across a range of altitudes and orbital planes, in addition
to a much larger number of smaller, harder-to-track fragments.

Because the fragments were not deliberately dispersed to a single controlled
orbit, they now cross the orbital paths used by many active satellites,
including large constellations in low Earth orbit. Fragments from events
like this remain a long-term collision risk precisely because they are
numerous, individually small (making some hard to track precisely), and
scattered across orbital planes that intersect heavily used altitude bands.

This kind of debris field is a good illustration of why debris mitigation
guidance treats collision avoidance as protecting the broader orbital
environment, not just the two objects in a given conjunction event — a single
destructive event years earlier continues to generate individual close
approaches with active missions long after it occurred.
