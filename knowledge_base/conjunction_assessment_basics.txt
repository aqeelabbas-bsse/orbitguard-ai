Conjunction Assessment Basics

A conjunction event occurs when two tracked objects in orbit are predicted to
pass close to each other. Two numbers matter most when judging how serious an
event is: miss distance (how close the objects come at their nearest point)
and relative velocity (how fast they are moving with respect to each other
at that point).

Miss distance alone is not a complete risk picture. A close pass at low
relative velocity is generally less urgent than the same distance at high
relative velocity, because higher relative velocity both increases the
chance of an actual intersection given tracking uncertainty and increases the
severity of a collision if one occurs, since impact energy scales with the
square of relative velocity.

As a general rule of thumb for screening purposes: passes under a few
kilometers deserve close review, passes in the tens of kilometers range
should be monitored and re-assessed as tracking data updates, and passes
beyond that are typically treated as low concern unless the object involved
is unusually large or the relative velocity is unusually high. These are
general operational heuristics, not fixed physical thresholds — actual risk
depends on the specific objects, their size, and the quality of the tracking
data available.
